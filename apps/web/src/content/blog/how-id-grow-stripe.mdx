Stripe is named in every AI answer about payments I checked, and cited far less often. On 6 November 2026 I asked ChatGPT search, Google AI Mode and Google's AI Overviews ten questions a buyer asks before choosing a payment provider. I ran them twice. All 59 answers that appeared named Stripe. Only 11, then 13, linked to a stripe.com page, and Google AI Mode linked to one in 1 of its 10 both times.

Stripe is hiring someone to fix that. Its listing for a [Head of AEO & SEO](https://stripe.com/jobs/search?gh_jid=8128634) was posted on 13 August 2026 and was still open on 6 November 2026. A second listing, for an [AEO and GEO Marketing Manager](https://stripe.com/jobs/search?gh_jid=7844214), has been open since 23 April 2026.

This is what I'd do in that job. It's built only from what anyone can see from outside: Stripe's public pages, search data and the two listings.

Key findings:

- Across two runs, 59 of 59 AI answers named Stripe, and 24 cited it. ChatGPT cited stripe.com in 8 of 10 answers both times, AI Overviews in 2 of 9 and then 4 of 10, and AI Mode in 1 of 10 both times (DataForSEO, US, 6 November 2026).
- In live US Google results on 6 November 2026, another country's English copy of stripe.com showed for 12 of the 40 searches I checked, from the ACH and CVV guides to Stripe's phone number. DataForSEO's database lists 8,454 such US searches, but live results matched its page for only 3 of the 40, so treat 8,454 as an upper bound.
- Seven US searches for Stripe's phone number, about 11,000 a month between them, show the New Zealand or Singapore sales form in Google's top four (live, 6 November 2026). Neither form has a phone number. The US contact page does.
- YouTube was cited in 11 of 17 AI Overviews in one run and 11 of 18 in the next, more than stripe.com (7, then 8). None of the videos came from Stripe's two YouTube channels.
- For "Accept a payment", docs.stripe.com sends ChatGPT's, Claude's and Perplexity's crawlers an 854-byte list of six links and no steps. A browser gets about 1,469 words, and Googlebot about 2,800.

Stripe doesn't publish its search traffic, so every visit number here is a third-party estimate. Read them for proportions.

### The copies are the same guide with British spelling and a shorter menu

The tags are right. Every copy lists all 77 addresses, itself included, and the US copy is marked `en`, `en-US` and `x-default`.

What's wrong is that the copies are nearly identical. The Australian ACH guide has 3,583 words to the US copy's 3,631. The body text is the same, with British spelling, a different date format and a header menu that drops US products such as Treasury and Issuing.

ACH is a US payment network. An Australian reader has no use for this guide, and a US reader who lands on the Australian copy gets a header menu without Treasury, Capital or Issuing, which are products they could buy.

Google says `hreflang` suits ["small regional variations with similar content, in a single language"](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions). The swap only works, though, when Google keeps each copy as its own page. When copies are this close, Google can fold them into one and pick which address to show. That would explain why a different copy wins for different searches.

Stripe can confirm it in Search Console. In URL Inspection, if the Google-selected canonical for the US ACH guide is an `/au/` or `/ae/` address, that's the cause.

### The fix: one English guide for topics that don't change by country

**The fix:** sort the 3,566 guides into three kinds, and give each kind the right set of copies.

1. Topics that only apply to one country (ACH, US state sales tax, Delaware companies, Australian GST). Keep that country's copy. Point every other English copy at it with a canonical tag, take them out of the `hreflang` set and the sitemap, and keep them reachable for the menu.
2. Topics that are the same everywhere (CVV, the Luhn algorithm, digital wallets). Publish one English page, marked `hreflang="en"` and `x-default`, with the translations beside it. Drop the 45 regional English copies the same way.
3. Topics that really differ by country (fees, local payment methods, tax registration). Keep the regional copies and make the difference visible in the body: local prices, local rules, local examples. That's the case `hreflang` was built for.

## docs.stripe.com sends AI crawlers 854 bytes where a browser gets 1,469 words

Stripe's docs are ahead of most companies on agents. There's an llms.txt with 742 lines, a Markdown copy of every page, and a robots.txt line that says `ai-train=yes, search=yes, ai-input=yes`.

The catch is in what that Markdown copy holds. The docs answer the normal page address with Markdown whenever the request asks for it: the user agents of OAI-SearchBot, GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot, ChatGPT-User and Claude-User, and even a browser that sends `Accept: text/markdown`. Googlebot and Bingbot get HTML with no such header, and a bigger page than a browser gets: about 1.84 MB and 2,800 words, against 1.68 MB and 1,469 words.

"Accept a payment" is one of the most important pages in the docs, and an AI crawler gets 854 bytes: a list of six variants to fetch, and no steps. A browser gets about 1,469 words.

The Markdown also carries an `X-Robots-Tag: none` header, which in the [robots rules Google documents](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots-meta-tag) means noindex and nofollow. Noindex on a Markdown copy is common, since it keeps duplicates out of Google, and none of the AI companies say whether their crawlers obey the header. So it's a small risk next to a robots.txt that says "search=yes", not a shown problem.

**The fix:** send the `noindex` only on the `.md` addresses, where it stops duplicates in Google, and never on the page's own address. Add a `Link` header with `rel="canonical"` to every Markdown response. On pages with variants, send the default variant in full, with the others listed below it.

To confirm it worked, request the page with OAI-SearchBot's user agent and check for no `X-Robots-Tag` and the full steps.

### The fix: one message, published everywhere the answers look

**The fix:** for each buyer question, decide the one answer Stripe wants repeated, then publish it in every format AI answers read.

1. The page on stripe.com, with the question as the title and the answer in the first two sentences.
2. A video on the Stripe channel with the same title, a five-minute walkthrough and the page linked in the description. "Stripe vs PayPal" and "Is Stripe safe?" first, since a reviewer currently owns both.
3. The docs, where the question is a developer's: "how to add Apple Pay to my website" is an AI Overview built from three YouTube videos, and the Stripe docs have the steps.
4. A LinkedIn and X post with the short version and the link, from the company account.
5. Reddit, as Stripe, only in the threads AI answers already cite and only where there's a real question to answer.

The order matters less than the sameness. When the page, the video and the post all say the same thing in the same words, the answer an AI writes from any of them says it too.

## Technical fixes for a site with 429,500 addresses

stripe.com's sitemap lists 7,279 addresses, and each comes with an average of 58 `hreflang` alternates: 422,256 more. That's about 429,500 addresses Stripe asks Google to keep track of (sitemap read 6 November 2026).

The guides are most of it. 3,566 guides become 274,582 addresses, and 160,470 of those are English copies for other regions.

### 1. Sitemap entries that redirect or say noindex

Of the first 600 sitemap addresses my crawl read (`seo crawl`, 6 November 2026), 213 redirect and 106 carry a noindex. Most are legal and region pages, because those come first in the sitemap, so the share across all 7,279 will be lower.

The docs sitemap has the same pattern. It lists 22 usage-based billing pages that carry `noindex`, including the [main usage-based billing page](https://docs.stripe.com/billing/subscriptions/usage-based), which now recommends Metronome for new integrations.

**The fix:** a sitemap should list only addresses that return 200 and can be indexed. Generate it from the same data that sets the canonical and robots tags, so the two can't disagree. If the usage-based billing pages are meant to stay out of Google, take them out of the sitemap. If they're not, remove the noindex.

### 2. Mixed-case addresses

Covered above. Any capital letter in a path returns a 200 page with a matching canonical. One 301 rule to lowercase fixes every page at once.

### 3. Structured data on the guides

The ACH guide's `Article` markup has `"author": null`, a generic social card as its image, no `dateModified` and "| Stripe" inside the headline. The page itself shows "Last updated May 8, 2026", which is the freshness signal the cited pages carry.

<BeforeAfter
  caption="The ACH guide's Article markup today, and with the fields that match what the page already shows (6 November 2026)."
  before={{ label: "Today", code: beforeSchema }}
  after={{ label: "After the fix", code: afterSchema }}

**The fix:** fill `dateModified` from the date the page already displays, set Stripe as the author organisation, use the guide's own diagram as the image and drop the brand from the headline. The docs have no structured data at all; a `TechArticle` with `dateModified` would do the same job there.

### 4. Large pages and es-419

A docs page is heavy. The HTML for "Accept a payment" is about 1.7 MB, and the crawl flagged six docs pages as large. Rendering isn't the problem: the text is in the HTML. Weight is, on a site with this many addresses.

38 of the 402 docs pages my crawl read list an `es-419` alternate. Google [doesn't support](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/specialty/international/localized-versions) `es-419` and ignores that line, so Latin American Spanish readers get whichever Spanish copy Google picks. Listing `es-MX`, `es-AR` and the other countries it serves would fix that.

### 5. Housekeeping

A made-up guide address returns a 404, which is right. Its canonical points at the homepage, and under `/au/` it points at `stripe.com/en-gb-us`. A 404 page shouldn't carry a canonical.

Some legal pages answer a US visitor with 26 words, "The content of this page is not applicable to the region where you are located", and a 200. Googlebot mostly crawls from the US, so that's the version Google sees. Those pages need a `noindex` for the regions they don't apply to, or to be out of the sitemap.

## Get the changes seen quickly

Google comes back to most of these pages on its own schedule, and with 429,500 addresses that can take a while. I'd submit every changed and new URL to [Index My Site](https://indexmysite.app/) as it ships, starting with the US copies of the guides that rank in the wrong country. Until Google recrawls them, it can't see the new canonicals.

## The lesson for any site with regional copies

If you publish the same page for several English-speaking countries, search for your top guides from one of them and look at the address that ranks. When it's another country's copy, your `hreflang` tags are probably fine, and your copies are too alike.

## How I ran this

Everything in this teardown came from SEO Skill, run by an agent. You can run the same audit on your own site.

**1. Connect the hosted MCP server.** It's free. You sign in with Google, and there's no password and nothing to install. In Claude Code:

```sh
claude mcp add --transport http seoskill https://seoskill.dev/mcp
```

Codex, Cursor and Claude on the web work too. The [MCP guide](/docs/mcp) has the steps for each one.

**2. Add a research key.** On your [account page](/account), save an Ahrefs or DataForSEO key. The key gives your agent search volumes, rankings and results pages, like the tables above. With DataForSEO it can also ask ChatGPT and Google's AI answers your questions and check who they cite.

**3. Install the CLI for crawling.** The crawl runs on your machine, so it can read any site:

```sh
npm i -g seo
seo login
```

To check a guide's regional copies, put their addresses in a file and crawl them as a list. On Stripe's ACH guide this flagged 45 of 46 English copies as duplicate content:

```sh
seo crawl --mode list --urls-file ach-copies.txt
```

**4. Ask your agent.** This is close to the prompt I used:

```text
Audit example.com for a US audience.
List the searches where Google ranks one of our
other countries' copies instead of the US page,
then check each one live from a US location.
Crawl the English copies of our top three guides
with seo crawl --mode list and compare them.
Request a page as OAI-SearchBot, ClaudeBot and
PerplexityBot and show the headers.
Ask ai_answers 10 buyer questions for us and our
top competitor, and list who gets cited instead.
Rank the fixes by how close each is to revenue.
```

On your own site, the hosted server adds the part I couldn't see from the outside: your real Search Console clicks and queries, and the canonical Google chose for each page. With those, you get measured numbers where this post has estimates.
