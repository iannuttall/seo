[Every](https://every.to/) is an AI media company. It publishes a daily newsletter and essays, sells a subscription, runs consulting for companies adopting AI, teaches cohort courses, and builds its own apps: Monologue, Cora, Spiral and Sparkle.

So Every makes money five ways from one audience. It also has a site with a Domain Rating of 77 and about 7,800 linking sites. In this teardown I look at how to use that site to sell more consulting, courses and apps.

## Most of Every's search traffic isn't buying anything

Ahrefs estimates every.to gets about 7,300 visits from Google a month. This map shows where they land.

About 9% of those visits reach a page that sells something. Most of the rest come from typos and off-topic searches, or from people searching for Every by name, who would find it anyway.

Every has had between about 7,800 and 45,000 visits a month from Google over the last year, depending on the month (Ahrefs estimates). Most of the swing is in two places. The first is the homepage, which Ahrefs put at about 9,700 visits a month on 2 November and about 1,500 two days later. Every still ranks first for "every", so Ahrefs changed its estimate, not Every's ranking, and those visits were never buyers.

The second is a handful of pages. The clearest example is a podcast page called "Introducing How Do You Use ChatGPT?". In September it was the biggest page on the site after the homepage, at about 10,800 visits a month. Most of them came from people typing "hatgpt", a typo of ChatGPT.

I'd keep the main page's address and copy, since Google already cites it, and add the client stories and a short "how an engagement works" section that the hub pages link back to.

## The model reviews are close to ranking

Every reviews each new AI model in its Vibe Check series, which is what someone searching for a new model wants to read. Their titles are written for the brand. They start with "Vibe Check:", and the GPT-5 review's title is just "GPT-5".

People search for the model name. "gpt 5.6 sol" gets about 2,500 US searches a month, and Every's review already ranks seventh for "gpt sol". "claude vs chatgpt" gets 35,000 (Ahrefs, November 2026), and Every has no page for it. The new content section below covers those comparisons.

## Three technical fixes, closest to revenue first

The crawl found three technical issues. I've put them in order of how close each one is to revenue, so the first one isn't the one that affects the most pages.

### 1. The guides send their canonical tag too late

A canonical tag tells Google which address is the main copy of a page. [Google only reads it in the head](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/consolidate-duplicate-urls) of the HTML. On every guide I tested, the tag arrives in the body, so Google ignores it and picks a main copy on its own. Ordinary posts are fine (22 of 22 tested).

<BeforeAfter
  caption="Where the canonical tag sits in the HTML of a guide page, compared with an ordinary post."
  before={{ label: "Guide page", html: before6 }}
  after={{ label: "Ordinary post", html: after6 }}
/>

Guides are the evergreen pages most likely to rank and to link to products, so they're the pages where this matters most.

**The fix:** output the canonical tag in the head of the guide template, as the post template already does. It should hold the full address of the page itself, such as `https://every.to/guides/compound-engineering`. Then check one guide in Search Console's URL Inspection, where the "User-declared canonical" should show that address.

### 2. Compound Engineering has four pages

Four every.to pages cover the same idea. The guide ranks second or third for "compound engineering" (1,600 US searches a month). The other three split the links that could push it to first, and Google has to work out which one to show.

Keep the original essay, since it has its own links and history, and have it send readers on to the guide. Then update the internal links and the sitemap so they only list the pages that stay.

### 3. 72 posts are published twice

Each of these posts has a twin: the same address with a long ID on the end. Both addresses return the full post, both name themselves as the canonical, and both are in the sitemap. So Google sees two equal pages and splits the links between them.

If the CMS needs the ID addresses for previews, they can stay, as long as each one redirects for everyone else. Some republished posts also have "-rerun" copies. For those, I'd update the original post and its date, or point the rerun's canonical tag at the original.

## How I ran this

Everything in this teardown came from SEO Skill, run by an agent. You can run the same audit on your own site.

**1. Connect the hosted MCP server.** It's free. You sign in with Google, and there's no password and nothing to install. In Claude Code:

```sh
claude mcp add --transport http seoskill https://seoskill.dev/mcp
```

Codex, Cursor and Claude on the web work too. The [MCP guide](/docs/mcp) has the steps for each one.

**2. Add a research key.** On your [account page](/account), save an Ahrefs or DataForSEO key. The key gives your agent search volumes, rankings and results pages, like the tables above. With DataForSEO it can also ask ChatGPT and Google's AI answers your questions and check who they cite.

**3. Install the CLI for crawling.** The crawl runs on your machine, so it can read any site:

```sh
npm i -g seo
seo login
```

**4. Ask your agent.** This is close to the prompt I used:

```text
Audit example.com and every site we own.
Crawl each one with seo crawl. Find where our sites
link to each other and which pages those links hit.
Use seo_research to find the searches our buyers
make, who ranks for them and whether we can compete.
Find the searches our competitors rank for and we
don't, and group them into pages we should write.
Ask ai_answers 10 buyer questions for us and our
top competitor, and say who gets cited.
Rank every fix by how close it sits to revenue.
```

On your own site, the hosted server adds the part I couldn't see from the outside: your real Search Console clicks and queries. With those, you get measured numbers where this post has estimates.

If you have more than one product, crawl the main site too and look at where it links to the others. The cheapest fixes in this teardown came from those links.
