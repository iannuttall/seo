## Compared with LaunchDarkly, Statsig …
→ /compare/posthog-vs-launchdarkly.md`

AI answers already put PostHog on the shortlist. On 6 November 2026 I asked ChatGPT search, Google AI Mode and Google's AI Overviews ten questions a buyer asks before choosing analytics, session replay, flags or LLM tooling. PostHog was named or cited in 24 of the 30 answers.

That matters more to PostHog than to most companies. Its own [handbook page on answer engine optimization](https://posthog.com/handbook/content/aeo-guide) says AI is "already our biggest acquisition channel by self-reported attribution", bigger than Google, YouTube, Reddit and social combined.

So this teardown is about turning mentions into citations and non-brand searches into visits. The brand, the docs, the handbook, the newsletter and the open source repo are already there. Most of the wins are about letting search engines and AI read what PostHog has written.

Key findings:

- Brand search brings about 8,700 of PostHog's estimated 10,700 monthly US Google visits (81%). Non-brand pages bring the rest, mostly from the blog and newsletter (DataForSEO estimate, November 2026).
- Every posthog.com page I fetched carries at least 857 KB of inline CSS before its body. The first heading starts after 1.2 MB on all 1,107 pages with one, and 81 of 1,174 pages run past the first 2 MB, which is all Googlebot reads.
- Google's AI Overviews for "session replay" and "web analytics" cited PostHog's product pages in 4 of 4 live checks each. In the organic results below them, the session replay page was missing every time and the web analytics page showed only on mobile.
- For "best llm observability tool", none of three AI answers named PostHog, and PostHog had no page in the top 26 for six LLM evaluation searches worth about 2,300 US searches a month.
- 69 of the 98 newsletter posts on newsletter.posthog.com also live on posthog.com/newsletter, and both copies point their canonical at themselves.

PostHog doesn't publish its search traffic, so every visit number here is a third-party estimate. Read them for proportions.

## Every posthog.com page sends 857 KB of CSS before its first word

posthog.com is built with Gatsby, and every page I fetched inlines Gatsby's whole global stylesheet into the HTML: 857,296 bytes at the least. After it come about 300 KB of inline SVG icons for the desktop-style menus and windows. Only then does the page start.

I fetched 1,174 posthog.com addresses from the sitemap as Googlebot's smartphone user agent on 6 November 2026: all 320 API reference pages and a sample of every other section. The median page was 1,348,769 bytes. On all 1,107 pages with an H1, the H1 started between 1,219,664 and 1,765,118 bytes into the file.

That matters because [Google's Googlebot documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/googlebot) says Googlebot "crawls the first 2MB of a supported file type", that the limit "is applied on the uncompressed data", and that at the cutoff it "only sends the already downloaded part of the file for indexing consideration". 81 of the 1,174 pages run past 2 MB: 77 API reference pages, the changelog, the Learn page, the main feature flags docs page and the Hogpedia feature flags article.

The Hogpedia article is the clearest case. Its first sentence starts at byte 2,363,382, past the cut. The markup without the CSS and SVGs is 111,293 bytes.

### Live searches don't show whether the 2 MB cut costs PostHog anything

I tested it with live US Google searches on 6 and 7 November 2026, desktop and mobile. The results don't line up with the 2 MB line, so the cut is a risk, not a known loss.

- Both Hogpedia articles are indexed. On 7 November, `site:` searches for posthog.com/hogpedia/feature-flags and posthog.com/hogpedia/session-replay returned each article on desktop and mobile. Hogpedia went live on 2 October 2026, and both articles came in the [same commit](https://github.com/PostHog/posthog.com/commit/dbfd3fd0) with no edits since.
- None of the feature flags article's own text came back, and all of it sits past 2 MB. Exact sentences from it (6 and 7 November), and three words and phrases that appear only in its text searched within its address (7 November), returned no posthog.com page in any check.
- All of the session replay article's text sits inside the limit, from byte 1,472,236. Its opening sentence returned the article first on mobile on 6 November, when the AI Overview also cited it. On 7 November its text brought the article back in 4 of 6 mobile checks and none of 7 desktop checks.
- The feature flags docs page points the other way. Two code names that appear only past byte 2.4 million in its HTML, searched within its address, returned the page in 5 of 6 checks on 7 November. Two that appear only inside the limit returned it in none of 4.

So Google holds some text from past 2 MB on at least one page, and the feature flags article's missing text may have another cause. The fix costs little either way.

### Link the Gatsby stylesheet to take 857 KB off every posthog.com page

**The fix:** in `gatsby-ssr.js`, which already uses `onPreRenderHTML`, replace the inline `<style data-identity="gatsby-global-css">` element with a `<link rel="stylesheet">` to the file Gatsby already serves at the same address (856,571 bytes, `/styles.48cdbcddf4d2a16b1b37.css`). Then move inline SVGs over about 20 KB, such as the 1 MB infobox hedgehog, into image files.

That takes about 857 KB off every page and puts the H1 near byte 390,000 on a typical page. 69 of the 81 pages over 2 MB would come back inside the limit. The 12 that wouldn't are ten API reference pages, the changelog and the feature flags docs page.

A linked stylesheet costs a browser one more request. posthog.com already serves that file with an ETag and `must-revalidate`, so on later pages the browser checks it is unchanged instead of downloading 857 KB again.

To confirm it worked, fetch the 81 pages again and check each is under 2 MB with its H1 in the first 500 KB. After Google recrawls, search the Hogpedia feature flags sentence again.

## PostHog has 81 comparison pages, and five of the six I checked missed page one

PostHog's sitemap lists 81 comparison pages under posthog.com/compare, from Amplitude alternatives to PostHog vs LaunchDarkly. The one that ranks is the one AI answers cite: the Amplitude alternatives page was fifth on desktop and mobile, and the AI Overview cited it in both live checks.

The other five I checked didn't reach page one. In live US results on 6 November 2026, PostHog's comparison page for Mixpanel alternatives was 12th on mobile and missing on desktop. The pages for Sentry, LaunchDarkly and Langfuse alternatives weren't in the top 26, and "google analytics alternatives" (590 US searches a month) showed no posthog.com page, though PostHog has a page called Google Analytics alternative.

None of the 81 has a Markdown copy (all 81 `.md` addresses returned a 404), and PostHog's llms.txt links none of them. It has 4,616 links to docs pages and none to a comparison, blog, newsletter, handbook or Hogpedia page at all. ChatGPT found the open source analytics comparison anyway, once. An agent that only reads llms.txt won't find it there.

**The fix:** add Markdown copies and llms.txt entries for the comparison pages first, then the blog and newsletter. Put a dated two-sentence answer at the top of each comparison page, the way the Amplitude page already opens.

## A PostHog significance calculator could rank for 2,900 searches a month

"statistical significance calculator" gets 2,900 US searches a month at a difficulty of 26, and "ab test calculator" 480 more (DataForSEO, November 2026). The results are calculator sites and a few testing tools; Statsig was third on desktop for "ab test calculator". PostHog was in neither.

## Housekeeping: shared titles, Article markup and nine README redirects

Thirteen newsletter category pages, such as posthog.com/newsletter/engineering, share one title: the word "Posts" and the brand name. My crawl of 201 pages found 110 with a title another page also uses. Each category page should name its category.

199 of those 201 pages had no structured data. I'd add `Article` markup to blog and newsletter posts, with the author, publish date and `dateModified`.

PostHog's GitHub README, on a repo with 40,675 stars, links nine posthog.com addresses that now redirect, such as /about to /why and /data-pipelines to /cdp. Pointing them at the final address saves a hop on every visit and every crawl.

## The lesson for Gatsby and other static sites: check where your first heading starts

Open your biggest page, view the source and search for your first heading. If it starts more than a megabyte in, the framework is putting its styling ahead of your words, and Googlebot reads only the first 2 MB.

## How I ran this

I build SEO Skill and Index My Site, and everything in this teardown came from SEO Skill, run by an agent, plus direct DataForSEO calls for the live results. You can run the same audit on your own site.

**1. Connect the hosted MCP server.** It's free. You sign in with Google, and there's no password and nothing to install. In Claude Code:

```sh
claude mcp add --transport http seoskill https://seoskill.dev/mcp
```

Codex, Cursor and Claude on the web work too. The [MCP guide](/docs/mcp) has the steps for each one.

**2. Add a research key.** On your [account page](/account), save an Ahrefs or DataForSEO key. The key gives your agent search volumes, rankings and results pages, like the tables above. With DataForSEO it can also ask ChatGPT and Google's AI answers your questions and check who they cite.

**3. Install the CLI for crawling.** The crawl runs on your machine, so it can read any site:

```sh
npm i -g seo
seo login
seo crawl https://example.com/ --max-pages 600
```

**4. Ask your agent.** This is close to the prompt I used:

```text
Audit example.com for US search and AI answers.
Fetch our 500 biggest pages as Googlebot and report
where the first heading starts and how many pages
run past 2 MB.
Ask ai_answers 10 buyer questions and list which
of our pages get cited, and who is cited instead.
Check our product pages live for their head terms,
desktop and mobile, and note when the AI Overview
cites a page that doesn't rank.
Rank the fixes by how close each is to revenue.
```

On your own site, the hosted server adds what I couldn't see from outside: your real Search Console clicks and queries, and the canonical Google chose for each page.
