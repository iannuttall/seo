[Ben's Bites](https://www.bensbites.com/) is Ben Tossell's AI newsletter. It goes out on Tuesdays and Thursdays to more than 118,000 subscribers, and it makes money from sponsors: one main ad and one tool placement in each send. Its sponsor page prices the main ad at about a $48 CPM, so each new subscriber adds revenue to every send for as long as they stay.

For a newsletter, that's the job search has to do: bring in readers who haven't heard of it yet. Ben is also changing the newsletter right now. In [Finding my thing](https://www.bensbites.com/p/if-you-feel-behind-right-now-dont) he says the format, topics and posting days will all vary until the end of the year ("I'm in experimental mode"). Search is the channel that keeps bringing in new readers whatever the format, as long as the pages they land on still exist.

## Ben's best X posts could be his best search pages

Ben has more than 200,000 followers on X, and his best posts there are guides on the subjects people search for. They live on x.com, where they bring in a spike of readers and then stop.

His X Article "How I code with agents, without being 'technical'" went out on New Year's Eve 2025 and has more than 4 million views and 24,000 bookmarks (November 2026). 28 sites link to it on x.com (Ahrefs). A week later he republished it on the newsletter, where 12 sites link to it, and there's a third copy on the cookbook. None of the three gets measurable search traffic.

His follow-up, "What you need to know to build with coding agents, if you're not technical", got about 580,000 views in January. That's the "Become Technical in 1 Day" guide on the cookbook. Each of its sections answers a search beginners make, and the cookbook also has four short glossary pages that its homepage doesn't link to.

"what is a terminal" and "what is bash" have difficulties of 5 and 7. Reddit threads, Wikipedia, Apple's manual and programming tutorials rank for them now, all written for people who already code. Ben writes for people who don't code but work with agents every day, and nobody on page one writes for them.

I'd split the guide into one post per question, each with a title that uses the words people search, and keep the full guide as the hub that links to them. Then I'd use the same pattern for every post that does well on X:

1. Post on X as now. That's where the spike comes from.
2. Within a week, publish the long version on bensbites.com, one post per question it answers. Substack can publish a post to the web without emailing it.
3. Send a short newsletter issue that links to the guide, instead of another full copy, so Google has one page to rank.
4. Reply to the X post with a link to the guide, so people who find the post later end up on Ben's own site.

The views on an X post come in the first few days. A page that ranks for "what is a terminal" is in front of about 1,100 searchers a month (Ahrefs, US), every month, and they're the people Ben writes for.

## One domain instead of seven

Ben's Bites is spread across a lot of addresses: the newsletter on www.bensbites.com, the catalog, the cookbook, a sessions site, a partner page, and the old bensbites.co and news.bensbites.com. Search engines treat each subdomain as its own site, so every one starts from zero, needs its own Search Console property and has to earn its own links.

The newsletter has the authority. Ahrefs counts about 4,000 sites linking to www.bensbites.com, against six for the cookbook. Everything new should go there, and every old address should end up there.

Substack decides how far this can go. It needs www.bensbites.com pointed straight at its servers, with Cloudflare's proxy off ([Substack's setup guide](https://support.substack.com/hc/en-us/articles/360051222571-How-do-I-set-up-my-custom-domain-on-Substack)), so Ben can't serve a folder like /guides from anything else on that domain. That's probably why the cookbook and sessions sites ended up on subdomains. Inside Substack, the guides go where the posts go, under /p/.

For search, that's fine. Every post's full text is in the HTML, and each post has a canonical tag and NewsArticle structured data. What Substack doesn't offer is control over templates and redirects, an llms.txt or a Markdown copy of each post (the AI search section below has what each AI crawler gets).

Those gaps are much smaller than splitting the guides across another domain, so I'd keep everything on Substack. If Ben later wants that control, the move is a site he runs himself on www.bensbites.com, with the same /p/ addresses, rather than another subdomain.

The first kind is a page that says what the newsletter is. "ai newsletter" and "best ai newsletter" go to a homepage or newsletter page whose title names the category. That's the homepage title fix above. TLDR also has a page for each of its newsletters, and its data one is 10th for "data engineering news".

### Coding agent comparisons, from the Vibe Coding Deathmatch

Ben's most linked post on X is the "Vibe Coding Deathmatch" from March 2025, a video of Bolt, Replit, Cursor and Windsurf building the same project (Replit won). Ahrefs counts 1,731 sites linking to it, more than any other post of Ben's. The newsletter write-up, "AI coding tools: Head-to-head", ranks for nothing in Ahrefs, and neither does "What AI coding tool should you use?".

These searches have grown since then, and their difficulty is still low. "cursor vs claude code" gets 6,100 searches a month at a difficulty of 5, and a post on someone's Substack ranks sixth. Another Substack post is sixth for "claude code vs codex". I'd make one hub and one page per pair, each from Ben's own test, with the verdict in the first two sentences.

## How I ran this

Everything in this teardown came from SEO Skill, run by an agent. You can run the same audit on your own site.

**1. Connect the hosted MCP server.** It's free. You sign in with Google, and there's no password and nothing to install. In Claude Code:

```sh
claude mcp add --transport http seoskill https://seoskill.dev/mcp
```

Codex, Cursor and Claude on the web work too. The [MCP guide](/docs/mcp) has the steps for each one.

**2. Add a research key.** On your [account page](/account), save an Ahrefs or DataForSEO key. The key gives your agent search volumes, rankings and results pages, like the tables above.

**3. Install the CLI for crawling.** The crawl runs on your machine, so it can read any site:

```sh
npm i -g seo
seo login
```

**4. Ask your agent.** This is close to the prompt I used:

```text
Audit example.com and every domain we have used.
Crawl each one with seo crawl, including old domains
and subdomains. Check where each old address redirects.
Crawl the old URLs the Wayback Machine has saved.
Use seo_research to find the searches new readers
make and who ranks for them, and the searches
our competitors rank for that we don't.
Ask AI assistants ten questions our readers ask
with seo research ai_answers, for us and our
biggest competitor.
Rank every fix by how close it sits to revenue.
```

On your own site, the hosted server adds the part I couldn't see from the outside: your real Search Console clicks and queries. With those, you get measured numbers where this post has estimates.

If your newsletter has moved platform or domain, crawl the old addresses too. In this teardown, that's where the traffic went.
